## 1. Total Turtle profits: CONFLICTING (four different figures, each with an identifiable origin)

**A. "$175 million in five years": originates with Turtle Russell Sands's course marketing.**
- **Sands's own sales pages** (TradeWins Publishing; Sands has sold the rules in seminars since 1992):
  - https://tradewins.com/russell-sands/turtle-workshop-home-study-course/ : "Over the next five years, the Turtles earned some $35,000,000 ... as their share of the $175 million in profits they made for their teacher". The same page claims this is "all publicly documented in The Wall Street Journal".
  - https://tradewins.com/russell-sands/the-turtle-secret-supercharged-on-stock-options/ : "From 1984 - 1989, despite the '87 crash ... banked over $175 million in profits for Dennis".
- **Check on Sands's WSJ claim:** the 5 Sept 1989 WSJ page reproduced on turtletrader.com gives an 80% return figure and no dollar total. I could not see any continuation page.
- **Investopedia, original 2008 version** ("Turtle Trading: A Market Legend", by Michael Carr; text preserved in reposts such as trade2win 2017 and idoc.pub): "According to former turtle Russell Sands, as a group, the two classes of turtles Dennis personally trained earned more than $175 million in only five years."
  - This byline is the newsletter writer Michael Carr (contact "Dunn Warren"). As far as I can tell he is not the Turtle Mike Carr, although one blog conflates them.
- **Investopedia, current version** (updated 4 Sept 2026, https://www.investopedia.com/articles/trading/08/turtle-trading.asp): the attribution to Sands has been dropped. It now reads "The two classes of turtles Dennis personally trained earned more than $175 million in only five years." and "reportedly earning over $175 million in five years."
- **Wikipedia "Richard Dennis"** (cites the Investopedia article): "When his experiment ended five years later, his Turtles reportedly had earned an aggregate profit of $175 million."
- **Chain:** Sands, then Investopedia, then Wikipedia. Every link is secondary to Sands's sales copy.
- **"$175 million in four and a half years":** I found this in no primary source. It appears to blend the $175M figure with Faith's and the WSJ's "four and a half years", which were said about the 80% return (item 2).

**B. "Over $100 million": Covel's site and Faith.**
- turtletrader.com homepage: "...watched them generate over $100 million in profits."
- turtletrader.com/thebet/ : "...the group generated over $100 million in profits."
- Covel's own site is not consistent. turtletrader.com/richard-dennis-turtle-want-ad/ (updated 19 Nov 2026) says: "By the time the experiment ended, the group had generated over $175 million in profits."
- Covel's book, p. 117, reports an online chat-forum post saying the whole group "made around $100 million". This is a forum claim that Covel repeats; he does not endorse it as data.
- Faith, *Way of the Turtle*, p. 223: the Turtles went on "to earn more than $100 million for him over the next four years".
- Faith's publisher blurb: "...an average return of over 80 percent per year and profits of more than $100 million."

**C. Dennis's own figure: "$150 million / $110 million".**
- Exact wording, quoted by Covel p. 133: "Tons. I think they grossed $150 million and we made $110 million. We started out paying them ten percent."
- **Correction to the brief:** this was not said to Covel. Covel's endnote 9 for chapter 8 (p. 233) cites the "Collins audiotape interview with Richard Dennis", Art Collins's 2005 audio interview.
- The wording is "they grossed", not "gross profits of about". "We made $110 million" plausibly means Dennis's side kept about $110M of about $150M gross. That reading is my interpretation, not his.
- Wikipedia's "The Complete TurtleTrader" article follows this figure: "The Turtles would go on to gross over $150 million in four years."

**D. Side conflict: the Turtles' share of profits.**
- Covel p. 7 says the Turtles were to get 15% and Dennis 85%.
- Dennis (above) says "ten percent".
- Sands's numbers ($35M of $175M) imply 20%.

## 2. Annual returns: VERIFIED. The original source is the WSJ in 1989; Faith's book wording differs slightly from what the brief assumed.

- **WSJ, 5 Sept 1989**, Stanley W. Angrist, "Winning Commodity Traders May Be Made, Not Born" (scan: https://www.turtletrader.com/images/wsj_turtle_article.pdf):
  - "Over the past 4 1/2 years, a group of 14 commodity traders he taught earned an average annual compound rate of return of 80%."
  - Table footnote: "Returns overstate performance because traders paid small or no commissions and no management fees through first quarter of 1988."
  - The table's individual compound returns run from 124.1% (Stig Ostgaard) down to 38.9% (Michael Carr).
  - Dennis quote in the same article: "Trading was even more teachable than I imagined ... almost humbling."
- ***The Original Turtle Trading Rules*** (OriginalTurtles.org, 2003; Curtis Faith "with help from many others"):
  - "...over the next four years, they [some versions: we] earned an average annual compound rate of return of 80%."
- **Faith, *Way of the Turtle* (2007), pp. xx–xxi:**
  - "Over four and a half years the Turtles earned an average return of over 80 percent per year." The book says "average return", not "compound".
  - Preface p. xviii, about himself: "more than 100 percent returns for the four-plus years of the Turtle program".
- **"Doubled their money every year": the source is Dennis in Schwager's first book, *Market Wizards* (1989), not *The New Market Wizards*.**
  - Schwager asks: "What were the results?"
  - Dennis replies: "We dropped three people who didn't do well. The other twenty, however, have averaged about 100 percent profit per year."
  - One 2003 forum transcription omits "about" and puts it at p. 96. Page numbers vary by edition.
- **Covel p. 7:** "Many of his students went on to make 100 percent or more per year over four years."
- **Assessment:** the two figures do not contradict each other. 80% is a compound (geometric) figure from Barclay-type track records with fee caveats. "About 100 percent" is Dennis's oral and probably arithmetic average. Present them as "about 80% a year compounded (WSJ 1989); Dennis himself claimed about 100% a year."

## 3. 1987–88 losses, the Drexel funds, April 1988, and "100 times our risk": VERIFIED (Covel); dollar totals CONFLICT across press

**The Drexel funds (verified).** LA Times, 30 Aug 1988:
- Dennis launched two public funds "last year" (that is, 1987) "in conjunction with Drexel Burnham Lambert Inc."
- They lost "more than 50% of their $115 million combined initial value".
- Dennis "suspended trading last April on the Richard J. Dennis Preferred Futures Funds I and II".
- LA Times, 28 May 1988 (Bill Sing): "Dennis I and Dennis II, halted trading in the past two months after losing about half of their investors' money."

**The October 1987 crash.**
- NYT, 28 Oct 1987 (Julia M. Flynn): his firm "lost $10 million last week". Dennis: "I slept like a baby."
- LA Times, 30 Aug 1988: C&D Commodities "reportedly lost $10 million".

**Covel's April 1988 figures (verified wording), p. 129.**
- "The game changed for Dennis when his losses were 55 percent in April 1988."
- The Turtles "typically lost 10 to 12 percent each for that month (see Appendix)."
- Table 8.1 (p. 130, source "Barclays Performance Reporting"): Apr-88 = -55.49%. Equity was about $113.0M in Mar-88 and $40.0M in Jun-88.
- Covel contradicts himself slightly on p. 132: "Dennis's students did not lose 50 percent in April 1988; he did."
- **Precision caveat:** Covel's Appendix IV gives individual Turtle results for April 1988. They range from +5.40% (Proctor) to -18.30% (Cheval):
  - Carr -13.90%, Cavallo -4.90%, DiMaria -12.10%, Gordon -0.21%, Lu 0.00%
  - Ostgaard -13.91%, Parker -10.71%, Rabar -9.99%, Seidler 0.00%, Shanks -13.30%
  - So "10–12%" is a rough median, not a uniform figure.

**Mike Shannon (verified; this is Covel's own interview, no endnote), p. 133.**
- Context: Turtles were allowed "one, possibly two, units" of S&P futures while Dennis traded "ten or fifteen units".
- Exact quote: "We calculated one day that his risk was probably one hundred times greater than the risk we were taking."
- The brief's paraphrase ("about 100 times our risk") is fine. The exact words are "one hundred times greater than the risk we were taking".

**Jim DiMaria.**
- p. 129: "All of a sudden it's over. That's how fast it was. They came in Monday morning and said, 'Friday, we're done.'"
- p. 130, Covel's paraphrase, sourced to Greg Burns, Chicago Sun-Times 1989: DiMaria said personal trading losses did not force Dennis's hand; it was Dennis's money, and he just didn't want to do it anymore.
- p. 135: "I would never have left. Unless they made it that I couldn't make money somehow."

**Dollar-loss figures disagree across the press:**

| Source | Figure |
|---|---|
| Time, 17 Oct 1988 (Stephen Koepp) | The two funds "lost an estimated $50 million in the past year, or nearly 50% of their value" |
| BusinessWeek, 7 Apr 1997 (Greg Burns) | In 1987–88, trading up to $159M, "his net loss after fees was $60 million" |
| LA Times/Bloomberg, 4 Oct 2000 | Funds "lost as much as $20 million after the stock market crashed in 1987" |
| Wikipedia | $10M on Black Monday; "$50 million reportedly lost in 1987–1988" (citing Time) |

**UNVERIFIED:** one secondary summary says Dennis told Schwager the accounts were down "just under 49%". I did not see that page myself.

## 4. End of the program (spring 1988) and the explanations: VERIFIED (who said what); CONFLICTING (why)

**How the Turtles were told.** Covel's two versions differ:
- p. 129: "Dennis sent a fax telling the Turtles that the program had been scuttled."
- DiMaria, same page: "They came in Monday morning and said, 'Friday, we're done.'"

**Timing.**
- Covel: "In early 1988"; the Appendix IV track records end April 1988.
- Faith (Stocks & Commodities, Jan 2011): he traded "for four and a half years, which was essentially as long as the program lasted". From January 1984 that means about mid-1988.
- Wikipedia: Dennis withdrew from managing others' money "in the spring of 1988".
- The Turtle program ended months before Dennis's public retirement announcement in late August 1988 (item 5).

**Explanations, by person:**
- **Curtis Faith** (interview in *Technical Analysis of Stocks & Commodities*, January 2011 issue, conducted 8 Nov 2010; https://traders.com/Documentation/FEEDbk_docs/2011/01/Interview.html):
  - "The program was disbanded when Rich got into some legal troubles with some stuff he was doing separate from the Turtles. His lawyers advised him to discontinue trading."
  - This is **not** in *Way of the Turtle*. A text search of the book found no explanation of why the program ended.
- **Covel (narrator), p. 129:** "The reason for the Turtle program shutdown has never been officially defined". He cites:
  - the 55% April loss;
  - the Drexel funds closing with big losses;
  - "his father had recently died".
- **An unnamed Turtle** (Covel p. 129): blamed "family issues".
- **"Other Turtles"** (Covel p. 130): the program "was ended as a consequence of the Drexel meltdown".
- **Mike Cavallo** (Covel's paraphrase, pp. 135–136): Dennis shut it down because of problems from the 1987 crash. Covel adds "(Dennis's April 1988 performance was a big reason, too.)"
- **DiMaria:** see item 3. Dennis simply didn't want to do it anymore.
- **Dennis himself:** retiring to pursue politics (item 5).
- **Dennis & Co. spokesman Mark Walsh** (LA Times, 30 Aug 1988): the decision to quit was not related to the two funds' losses, which he blamed on "choppy markets--the worst markets we've seen in 10 years". This refers to Dennis quitting trading, not specifically to the Turtles.

**Chronology caution on Faith's "legal troubles".** The earliest investor suit I found reported is 21 Sept 1989 (Chicago Tribune, item 6). That is after the spring-1988 shutdown, although disputes and regulatory problems over the funds may have begun earlier. Treat Faith's explanation as one participant's recollection from 22 years later.

## 5. LA Times, 30 Aug 1988: VERIFIED

**Article:** "'Prince of Pit' Dennis Quits for Politics", https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1988-08-30-fi-1350-story.html. No individual byline.

**Key lines (short):**
- Lede: Dennis, "known as the 'Prince of the Pit' before last October's stock market crash sent his commodities trading business into a nose dive, said he was quitting to concentrate on politics."
- His fortune, "reportedly around $200 million" made in 18 years, would launch full-time involvement in "public policy and political endeavors."
- Dennis's statement: "There is only so much satisfaction and utility to the making of money"; "As I approach 40 years of age, I believe it is time for me to take on this new set of challenges."
- He said he would not run for office.

**Losses (paraphrased):**
- C&D reportedly lost $10M in the crash.
- The Drexel-launched funds lost over 50% of a $115M combined initial value.
- Trading was suspended in April.
- The funds resumed on 3 Aug after an investor vote. Each had lost about 3.5% when trading "was halted for good on Friday", which was 26 Aug 1988.
- Spokesman Walsh blamed "choppy markets".

**Investor numbers:** none in this article. The "nearly 6,000 investors" figure comes from the NYT in 1990 (item 6). A secondary summary says the Washington Post (1 Sept 1991) reported about 6,000 customers and about $100M. I did not see that article.

**Related coverage:**
- **Time, 17 Oct 1988** (content.time.com, "The Crash, One Year Later"):
  - says Dennis "decided last month" to quit for politics;
  - funds down about $50M, nearly 50%;
  - his philosophy: "an idiosyncratic mix of economic free markets and liberal social policies".
- **LA Times, 28 May 1988:** covers the fund suspensions (quoted in item 3).
- **NYT, 26 Apr 1988** (Julia Flynn Siler, "A Commodity Trader Fares Poorly in Funds"): cited by Covel (Dennis: "What should give investors confidence is the overall record I've compiled in my 18-year career"). I could not read the article itself.
- **Chicago Tribune / NYT stories from 27–31 Aug 1988:** NOT LOCATED. The Tribune pages exist on newspapers.com behind a paywall.
- **Schwager's *Market Wizards* chapter intro:** a follow-up interview dealt with Dennis's "large losses in his public fund trading". Schwager adds: "A month after our last conversation, Dennis announced that he was retiring from trading".

## 6. Lawsuits and the settlement: settlement VERIFIED; the "deviated from his rules" allegation only PARTLY VERIFIED

**Settlement.** NYT, 21 Nov 1990, Sec. D p. 19, "Judge Approves Dennis Pact" (Reuters), https://www.nytimes.com/1990/11/21/business/judge-approves-dennis-pact.html:
- A federal judge in Manhattan "yesterday", i.e. 20 Nov 1990, approved a multimillion-dollar settlement with investors who "said they had been misled by his company and lost half their outlay."
- "United States District Judge Milton Pollack agreed to a settlement in which nearly 6,000 investors will share $2.5 million almost immediately and get half of Dennis & Company's trading profits in the next three [years]". The snippet I saw was cut off after "three".
- **Year:** 1990.
- **Date errors elsewhere:**
  - Wikipedia's citation gives the date as "1990-11-02", although its own URL says 1990/11/21.
  - Covel's endnote (p. 233) gives "November 11, 1990".
  - Both are wrong; the date is 21 Nov 1990.

**No admission of wrongdoing.**
- Covel p. 132: "Under the settlement, Dennis and his firms did not admit any wrongdoing."
- LA Times/Bloomberg, 4 Oct 2000: "In 1990 he admitted no wrongdoing in a settlement with investors."
- Wikipedia: "without admitting or denying any wrongdoing".
- I could not see the NYT's own wording on this point.

**The allegation.**
- Covel p. 131: "Lawsuits soon followed as his former clients in the Drexel funds argued that Dennis had deviated from his own rules."
- Wikipedia: "investor complaints of his failure to follow his own rules".
- The contemporaneous press I could read frames it differently:
  - NYT 1990: investors said they were "misled".
  - Chicago Tribune, 21 Sept 1989 (a brief): a suit by Lola Flamm in federal court in Chicago claimed Dennis & Co. failed to "adequately disclose the level of risk" and the firm's past trading practice. Drexel allegedly breached its fiduciary and contractual duty by clearing the orders.
  - BusinessWeek, 7 Apr 1997: burned investors accused Dennis of being so distracted by politics that he failed to monitor trading, which he disputes. Plaintiffs called him "financially strapped" and "debt-ridden", and court records say he agreed to borrow $2.5M from his brother for the settlement.
- **Status:** "deviated from his rules" is Covel's and Wikipedia's characterization. The court filings themselves are UNVERIFIED.

## 7. Covel: "not pure trend following": VERIFIED

**Covel p. 132, exact sentence:** "His losses were not the result of pure trend-following trading, and there is no way to know exactly what he was doing differently."

**Context:**
- It follows "Uncontrollable? Dennis's students did not lose 50 percent in April 1988; he did."
- It also follows Covel's point that the Turtles' strategy "was not the same strategy their teacher was using".

**Supporting voices in Covel:**
- Larry Hite (paraphrased): Dennis's trading "did not make sense", since no single market move explains the losses.
- David Cheval: "The issue is whether he followed his system when he took public money."
- Shannon's "one hundred times" quote (item 3).

## 8. Backtests ("drop after 1986", "flat 1996–2009"): source traced to a self-published blog; exact wording UNVERIFIED

**Wikipedia's sentence** ("Richard Dennis"): "The result of such back-test shows a drastic drop in performance after 1986, and even a flat performance from 1996 to 2009."

**What it cites:**
- "Were the Turtles just lucky?", automated-trading-system.com/turtles-just-lucky/.
- The citation's archive link is dated 2021-01-22. The earliest Wayback capture is 11 Mar 2010.
- The site was the self-published trading blog "Au.Tra.Sy", which I believe was Jez Liberty's. That attribution comes from my knowledge of the site; the post text was not retrieved.
- The domain now redirects to an unrelated gambling site.
- I could not read the archived post because web.archive.org is blocked here, so its exact wording, markets and rules are UNVERIFIED.
- Secondary sites (Invezz 2022; Forex Training Group 2021) repeat these figures, attributed loosely to "Trading Blox":
  - "returns of 216% between 1970 and 1986"
  - "10.5% from 1986-2009"
  - "completely flat between 1996 and 2009"
- The "216%" and "10.5%" figures were most likely annual growth rates from a Trading Blox software simulation, but that is unconfirmed.

**Best citable published equivalent: Anthony Garner, "Tuning up the turtle", *Active Trader*, Feb 2010, pp. 34–38.** A preview ran in *Futures & Options Trader*, Dec 2009 (PDF on the tradingblox.com forum).

*Setup:*
- Simulation in Trading Blox software.
- Rules: Turtle **System 1 only** (enter on a 20-day breakout, exit on a 10-day breakout, skip a signal after a winner with a 55-day failsafe, 2N stop, add units every ½N, unit caps of 4/6/10/12).
- Risk: 1% per unit instead of the original 2%.
- Markets: the 21 original Turtle markets, with the Euro in place of the D-mark, and French franc and T-bills omitted.
- Period: 1 Jan 1970 to 23 Sept 2009, starting with $1M, including interest, slippage and commissions.

*Results:*
- 72% compound annual growth rate over the full period.
- 66% maximum drawdown; the longest drawdown lasted 85 months.
- "since the early 1990s, the system has essentially been unprofitable."
- On the Trading Blox forum (Feb 2010), Garner said "the 20/10 Original Turtle Rules died a death a long time ago".
- Note: this dates the decline to "the early 1990s", not to 1986.

**Counterpoint.** Faith tested a simplified Turtle-style "Donchian Trend" system (breakout entries plus a trend filter) on 28 US futures markets from Jan 1996 to June 2006 (*Way of the Turtle*, p. 104):
- compound annual growth of 27.38%;
- maximum drawdown of 22.35%.
- So "flat 1996–2009" applies to the original System 1 rules as tested, not to trend following in general.

**Verdict:** safe to say "later backtests of the original rules (for example Garner, *Active Trader*, 2010) found the system made little or no money from the early 1990s on." The precise "after 1986" and "flat 1996–2009" figures rest only on a now-dead self-published blog.

## 9. *Trading Places*: dates and plot VERIFIED; no documented causal connection

**US release:**
- Wednesday 8 June 1983 (Paramount), wide release in 1,375 theaters (Wikipedia).
- AFI Catalog: Los Angeles and New York openings 8 June 1983; production ended 1 March 1983.
- Covel calls it "the spring 1983 classic movie", which is slightly inaccurate.
- Chinese Wikipedia's infobox gives 1983年7月8日, which is an error.

**The $1 bet:**
- AFI synopsis: Randolph argues environment (nurture) and Mortimer heredity (nature). They bet that Valentine could succeed in Winthorpe's role at the commodities brokerage Duke & Duke, where Winthorpe was managing director, and that Winthorpe would turn to crime. The stake is "the usual amount": one dollar.
- Wikipedia: the Dukes "settle their wager for $1".
- Baidu Baike: "以一美元做赌注".

**Chinese titles (Chinese Wikipedia infobox):**

| Region | Title |
|---|---|
| Mainland China | 《颠倒乾坤》 |
| Hong Kong | 《运转乾坤》 |
| Taiwan | 《你整我，我整你》 (also used by Apple TV Taiwan) |
| Singapore | 《偷龙转凤大老千》 |

Baidu, Douban and 1905.com also list the alternative titles 交换身份, 交易所 and 交易地点.

**Connection to the Turtle experiment: none documented.**
- Covel, ch. 3, pp. 29–30:
  - Screenwriter Herschel Weingrod "flatly said that he had never heard of Richard Dennis when his script was completed in October 1982".
  - Dennis, asked whether the film inspired the experiment: "Oh God, no! Actually I think the movie came after."
  - Turtle Mike Carr: "I never viewed it as anything other than coincidental."
  - Turtle Mike Shannon insisted it "had a freaking role".
  - Covel's own speculation: "at a bare minimum the movie must have been a catalyst". On p. 135 he calls the film "a fire-starter for the Turtle experiment".
- Covel also claims the film grossed over $100M before the experiment was planned. Treat this as doubtful: Chinese Wikipedia lists a US$90.4M gross and English Wikipedia $120.6M lifetime.
- Investopedia (2008) only draws the parallel ("in the same year the movie was released").
- Some blogs (for example the7circles.uk) say the experiment inspired the film. That is chronologically impossible: the script was finished in Oct 1982, the film released in June 1983, and the Turtle ads ran in late 1983.
- English Wikipedia credits the idea to co-writer Timothy Harris's tennis games with two wealthy, frugal brothers.
- **Verdict:** a parallel and a source of speculation, not a documented link.

## 10. Did Dennis return to trading? VERIFIED (yes; stopped again in 2000), with a conflict over the return year

**The return:**
- LA Times/Reuters, 27 Aug 1991 (https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1991-08-27-fi-1817-story.html): Dennis is "back in action after a three-year retirement". Dennis Trading Group, set up with his brother Tom, "started trading Aug. 1" (1991).
- BusinessWeek, 7 Apr 1997: in 1992, "when his equity climbed as high as $86 million, he lost $48 million". By 1997 he was trading by computerized system with $85M under management.
- **Conflict:** Covel (p. 146) says Dennis launched Dennis Trading Group with his brother Tom "in 1994". He reports roughly +63% a year compounded from 1994 to Sept 1998, including 1995 +108.9% and 1996 +112.7% (p. 150).

**Closing again in 2000:**
- LA Times/Bloomberg, 4 Oct 2000 (https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-oct-04-fi-31019-story.html):
  - the fund was closing and liquidating customer accounts after losing money most of the year (down 37% for the year);
  - assets peaked at $350M in June 1999 and were $79M at the close;
  - it had grown 27% a year compounded since May 1995;
  - "He returned to the money management business in 1991."
- Covel p. 151: Dennis Trading Group "ceased trading and liquidated customer accounts" on 29 Sept 2000, after hitting -50% in June and then -52%.
- Wikipedia: Dennis "managed funds for some time in the mid and late 1990s, closing these operations after losses in the summer of 2000."
