# B_people.md — Turtle Traders (Dennis / Eckhardt, 1983–88): people, quotes, numbers
Fact-check compiled 2026-11-24 for a Chinese-language history video.

## How this was checked (read first)
- Primary documents actually read (page images): WSJ, 5 Sept 1989 (Angrist) reprint PDF; NYT Magazine, 25 Apr 1976 (Bauer) ProQuest scan. Both hosted on Covel's turtletrader.com.
- Book wording verified by single-phrase "search inside" lookups in the Internet Archive / Open Library full-text index of scanned editions: Schwager, *Market Wizards* (NYIF 1989; Perennial 1990; HarperBusiness 1993); Schwager, *The New Market Wizards* (HarperBusiness 1992); Faith, *Way of the Turtle* (McGraw-Hill 2007). These are lending-restricted scans, so only ~100-character snippets were returned and **printed page numbers could not be retrieved**. Where a quote below is marked [verified], the quoted words were returned by that index. Longer passages come from text excerpts and are marked as such.
- Covel's *The Complete TurtleTrader* (2007) is not in that index. His wording was taken from his own site turtletrader.com, which reproduces the book text, plus a text excerpt of ch. 1 and ch. 3.
- Tools: Firecrawl had no credits; Google Books API was rate-limited; Exa failed (401) late in the session. Nothing below relies on an unsourced blog for an exact quote. Where only blogs or wikis carry a claim, this file says so.

## ITEM 1 — "We are going to grow traders just like they grow turtles in Singapore."
**STATUS: VERIFIED (speaker: Richard Dennis). Earliest printed sources found are both from 1989.**

(a) Jack D. Schwager, *Market Wizards* (New York Institute of Finance, 1989), Richard Dennis interview (chapter "Richard Dennis: A Legend Retires"; the interview was c.1988 according to secondary summaries). Snippets returned by the full-text index, identical in the 1989, 1990 and 1993 scans:
- Schwager (question): "...heard this group of traders referred to as the "turtles." I found that term somewhat amusing. What is the..."
- Dennis: "...going to grow traders just like they grow turtles in Singapore." I had visited a farm there and seen a huge vat with thousands of squirming turtles; that became my image of growing traders."
  (The words before "going to grow" were not returned. Covel prints the sentence as "We are going to grow traders just like they grow turtles in Singapore.")

(b) Stanley W. Angrist, "Winning Commodity Traders May Be Made, Not Born," *The Wall Street Journal*, Tue 5 Sept 1989, "Your Money Matters" column. Visually verified on the reprint at https://www.turtletrader.com/images/wsj_turtle_article.pdf :
> The students were called the "turtles". (Mr. Dennis, who says he had just returned from Asia when he started the program, explains that he described it to someone by saying, "We are going to grow traders just like they grow turtles in Singapore.")

- Which came first in print? Both are 1989 and I could not fix *Market Wizards*' publication month. No 1984–88 printing of the remark was found (UNVERIFIED that none exists).
- Faith quotes the WSJ sentence verbatim in his 2003 "Original Turtle Trading Rules" PDF.

**Wording variants (do not use as the "original"):**
- McGraw-Hill jacket copy for *Way of the Turtle* (2007), e.g. the Goodreads/Amazon description: "“We're going to raise traders just like they raise turtles in Singapore.” So trading guru Richard Dennis reportedly said to his long-time friend William Eckhardt nearly 25 years ago."
- WSJ, Dave Kansas, "The Tortoise and the Harried Trader" (May 2007; text as quoted on the EliteTrader forum): "While at a turtle farm in Singapore, the two men shook hands on the wager. Mr. Dennis reportedly blurted out: "We're going to raise traders like they raise turtles in Singapore."" This **conflicts** with the 1989 sources, where Dennis visited the farm earlier and later "described it to someone."
- Publisher marketing copy (Readings.com.au listing): "While sightseeing at a turtle farm, legendary commodities trader Richard Dennis bet Bill Eckhardt..." This is marketing, not a source.
- Wikipedia ("The Complete TurtleTrader") truncates it to "We are going to grow traders just like they grow turtles."
- George Anders, *The Rare Find* (2011): "(“We are going to grow traders just like they grow turtles in Singapore,” he told a friend.)"

**Name-origin disputes** (Covel's paperback Afterword, turtletrader.com/myths):
- Mike Shannon: "Our original name, in the first year of our existence, was the Disciples. Because it was the name, at the time, of a prominent street gang on Chicago's West Side, we agreed to go with the 'Turtle' idea."
- Lucy Wyatt Mattinen: the name traced back to Dennis's fondness for the 1960s band The Turtles.

**Recommendation:** Use the 1989 wording and attribute it to Dennis (*Market Wizards* / WSJ 1989). Do not say it was said to Eckhardt at the farm.

## ITEM 2 — Dennis: nickname, starting stake, wealth; Eckhardt: background; who believed what

### "Prince of the Pit" — VERIFIED
- Origin: the headline of Douglas Bauer, "PRINCE OF THE PIT," *The New York Times Magazine*, 25 Apr 1976, p. 200. Scan: https://www.turtletrader.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/Price_of_the_Pit.pdf . The deck reads: "Richard Dennis knows how to keep his head at the center of a shouting mob. That skill, and a shrewd understanding of the commodities market, has made him a multimillionarie [sic] at 26."
- Bob Tamarkin, *The New Gatsbys* (1985), has a chapter titled "Prince of the Pit": "Dubbed Prince of the Pit by the press, he traded huge volume" (index snippet).
- Variants: Faith 2007 uses "Prince of the Pits" (plural) [verified]; Bloomberg 2007 headline uses "Prince of Chicago Pit".

### Starting capital — CONFLICTING (the versions are compatible if read carefully)

|---|---|

Safest wording: he borrowed $1,600 (NYT 1976, from his father), and roughly $400 was left after buying a MidAmerica seat (WSJ 1989 "$400 grubstake"; the $1,200 seat price comes from Wikipedia / the *Market Wizards* paraphrase).

### Wealth in the early-to-mid 1980s — CONFLICTING estimates, none audited
- WSJ 1989: "an estimated $200 million or so in 18 years" (that is, as of 1989).
- Schwager 1992: "estimated at $200 million."
- Covel's book: "over $200 million" (per Wikipedia citing a Futures review).
- Faith: "several hundred million by his mid-thirties."
- Investopedia: "more than $100 million" by the early 1980s.
- Wikipedia lead: "reportedly made $350 million" (no citation on that sentence).
- In 1976 (NYT) he was worth "more than two" million at 26.

### William Eckhardt's background — VERIFIED
- University of Chicago News (14 July 2008): "Eckhardt holds two mathematics degrees, a master's from the University of Chicago (1970), and a bachelor's from DePaul University (1969)."; he "worked closely with ... Saunders Mac Lane"; "co-founded C & D Commodities with Richard Dennis in 1978, and they operated it together until 1987. Eckhardt established Eckhardt Trading Company in 1991."
- *Futures* magazine, Daniel P. Collins, 1 Mar 2011: he left U. Chicago and "foresook his nearly completed PhD in mathematical logic in 1973." Eckhardt: "In 1973 my advisor and I were having disagreements about the direction my dissertation was taking, and Rich suggested I take a little time off and come down to the floor to trade. I didn't go back."
- His Amazon author bio gives 1974 instead: "In 1974, after four years of doctoral research in Mathematical Logic at the University of Chicago, Eckhardt began trading for his own account at the Mid America Commodity Exchange."
- He and Dennis were high-school friends (Futures 2011; Dennis in *Market Wizards*).
- CAUTION: Faith's line "He looked and dressed like a professor of applied mathematics at the University of Chicago" [verified] describes his appearance, not his degree.
- CAUTION: Wikipedia/LOC give birth year 1955. That cannot be right alongside a 1969 B.A. Do not use it.

### Who believed what
- Dennis, *Market Wizards* (1989). Snippet: "...see what happens." He agreed. It was an intellectual experiment. We trained them as well as..." [verified]. Covel quotes the full passage: "My partner Bill has been a friend since high school. We have had philosophical disagreements about everything you could imagine. One of these arguments was whether the skills of a successful trader could be reduced to a set of rules, that was my point of view, or whether there was something ineffable, mystical, subjective, or intuitive that made someone a good trader. ... Finally, I said, 'Here is a way we can definitely resolve this argument. Let's hire and train people and see what happens.' He agreed. It was an intellectual experiment."
- WSJ 1989: the other side was "some of his associates" (Eckhardt is not named), who argued his skills were "ineffable, mystical, subjective or intuitive."
- Schwager 1992: "It was Dennis's contention that trading success could be taught, while Eckhardt scoffed at the idea." [verified] (Covel's site mislabels this passage as coming from *Market Wizards*.)
- Faith 2003: "Richard believed that he could teach people to become great traders. Bill thought that genetics and aptitude were the determining factors."
- Faith 2007: "Dennis believed he could transform almost anyone into a winning trader; Eckhardt believed it was a matter of nature, not nurture." [verified] Faith also writes: "I think the bet was a draw." [verified]
- Eckhardt's later statements:
  - *New Market Wizards* (1992): "The answer to the question of whether trading can be taught has to be an unqualified yes." ... "Anyone with average intelligence can learn to trade. This is not rocket science." [verified]
  - *Futures*, Jan 1992, answering "I thought you believed traders were born, not made": "That was (my) initial view, but after the turtle experience, I was forced to think otherwise".
  - CONFLICT — *Futures*, March 2011: "Various eyewitnesses to this episode disagree... I don't believe I ever advocated that trading cannot be taught, but if my memory is faulty on this point, I freely admit that... trading can be taught... All parties agree, however, there was no bet. Possibly an argument among hundreds of others, but no bet."

## ITEM 3 — Ads, applicants, interviews, test

### Which newspapers — CONFLICTING (the third paper differs)
- WSJ 1989: "In late 1983 and again in 1984, he placed ads in The Wall Street Journal, Barron's and the New York Times seeking people who wanted to be trained as traders. The job required that they move to Chicago, where they would receive a small salary and a percentage of any profits while Mr. Dennis taught them his methods."
- Faith 2003: "They took out a large ad advertising positions for trading apprentices in Barron's, the Wall Street Journal and the New York Times."
- Faith 2007 (Introduction): "they took out large ads in the Wall Street Journal, Barron's, and the New York Times announcing that Dennis was accepting applications ... The ad further stated that he would teach this group his trading methods and give each trainee a million-dollar trading account."
- Covel 2007 (ch. 1): "His firm, C&D Commodities, budgeted $15,000 for classified ads in the Wall Street Journal, Barron's, and the International Herald Tribune seeking trainees during late fall 1983 and 1984."
- Individual recollections: Jerry Parker saw it in the WSJ (Meb Faber podcast, 2017); Erle Keefer saw it in Barron's (Covel); Cheval's obituary (Bloomberg/SMH 2013) mentions "a three-line advertisement ... in The Wall Street Journal"; Investopedia mentions only the WSJ.
- Safe wording: the WSJ and Barron's plus at least one other major paper (NYT per WSJ 1989 and Faith; International Herald Tribune per Covel).

### Ad text
Only one verbatim text is published: Covel's reproduction. Its deadline shows it is the **1984 (second-round) ad**; Parker confirms "that specific ad is for the second group."
> Richard J. Dennis of C&D Commodities is accepting applications for the position of Commodity Futures Trader to expand his established group of traders. Mr. Dennis and his associates will train a small group of applicants in his proprietary trading concepts. Successful candidates will then trade solely for Mr. Dennis: they will not be allowed to trade futures for themselves or others. Traders will be paid a percentage of their trading profits, and will be allowed a small draw. Prior experience in trading will be considered, but is not necessary. Applicants should send a brief resume with one sentence giving their reasons for applying to: C&D Commodities 141 W. Jackson, Suite 2313 Chicago, IL 60604 Attn: Dale Dellutri. Applications must be received by October 1, 1984. No telephone calls will be accepted.

- The 1983 ad's exact text: UNVERIFIED.
- Faith's claim that the ad promised a "million-dollar trading account" does not appear in Covel's text.
- Covel adds: "Lost in the back pages of national dailies, the ad attracted surprisingly few respondents."

### Number of applicants — VERIFIED as roughly 1,000+
- WSJ 1989: "About 1000 applicants answered each ad." (note: per ad)
- Faith 2003: "over 1000 applicants"; Faith 2007: "Over 1,000 people had applied" [verified context].
- Covel: "the thousand-plus applicants."
- Investopedia: "thousands applied."

### Number interviewed — CONFLICTING (80 vs 40)
- 80: WSJ 1989 ("He selected 80 people to come to Chicago for interviews"); Faith 2003 ("Of these, he interviewed 80").
- 40: Faith 2007 ("only 40 applicants had been granted an audience with Dennis") [verified]; Faith, *Stocks & Commodities* interview (Jan 2011) ("one of the 40 people they interviewed"); WSJ 2007 (Kansas) ("culled the group down to 40"); *Market Wizards* per the7circles' paraphrase ("1,000 applications down to forty interviews and then hired ten"; verbatim not retrieved).

### True/false test — VERIFIED that it existed; question count CONFLICTING
- Covel (ch. 3, ~p. 34–35): "Candidates had to complete a 63-question true–false test." Covel also mentions essay questions and that applicants were asked for college entrance exam scores. The full list is at turtletrader.com/selection.
- Faith 2007: "I also had taken Richard's 40-question test (that was part of the application)"; "one of the questions on the preinterview questionnaire had been about our SAT scores."
- Investopedia prints 5 sample true/false items, e.g. "The big money in trading is made when one can get long at lows after a big downtrend." (false per the Turtle method).

## ITEM 6 — Training, trial month, account sizes

### Training length — VERIFIED
- First class: two weeks at the end of December 1983 (WSJ 1989; Faith 2003; Faith 2007: "the training would be held during the last two weeks of the year").
- Second class: one week. Faith 2007: "In fact, in the second year Rich and Bill hired a new crop of Turtles and trained them in only one week." [verified] The *Market Wizards* paraphrase agrees (two weeks in year one, one week in year two).

### Start and trial month — VERIFIED
- Faith 2003: "began trading small accounts at the beginning of January. After we proved ourselves, Dennis funded most of us with $500,000 to $2,000,000 accounts at the start of February."
- Faith 2007: "At the end of the trial trading period, which lasted one month, Rich evaluated our performance. Some Turtles received the full $1 million to trade, others were given smaller accounts, and still others were told to keep trading with the original account size. Rich gave me a $2 million account" [verified]
- Faith 2007 also writes: "The fact that none of the other Turtles followed those rules that first month..." [verified], and that on his heating-oil trade "my account was up $78,000" [verified].

### Account sizes after the trial — CONFLICTING
- "$1 million" each: WSJ 1989 ("When classroom work ended he gave each student $1 million in capital to trade"); Covel ("Each student received $1 million to trade after his classroom instruction. They were to get 15 percent of the profits"); Parker ("in January of 1984, we started trading with a million dollars").
- "$500,000 to $2,000,000": Faith 2003.
- "$250,000 to $2 million": Wikipedia (no citation) and Covel's DeNardo page.

### "Up to 12 contracts per market" in the trial month — UNVERIFIED
- It appears only in Wikipedia (uncited) and TraderLife (2018). I found it in no primary or book source.
- Possible confusion: 12 is the Turtle rules' maximum number of **Units** in one direction (Faith 2003 table: "Single Direction – Long or Short — 12 Units").
- Faith's own wording is only "small accounts" and "the original account size."

### End of the program
- Covel's Appendix IV performance tables run January 1984–April 1988.
- Hawksbill's disclosure: Dennis "decided to close all such managed accounts" in May 1988.

## ITEM 8 — Best Turtles took the entries; the worst and all those dropped did not
**STATUS: VERIFIED. Faith said it twice.**
- 2003, "The Original Turtle Trading Rules" (OriginalTurtles.org free PDF, ch. 4 "Entries", "Consistency"):
> The Turtles were told to be very consistent in taking entry signals, because most of the profits in a given year might come from only two or three large winning trades. If a signal was skipped or missed, this could greatly affect the returns for the year. The Turtles with the best trading records consistently applied the entry rules. The Turtles with the worst records, and all those who were dropped from the program, failed to consistently enter positions when the rules indicated.
- 2007, *Way of the Turtle*, appendix "Original Turtle Trading Rules" (≈ pp. 261–262; page numbers vary by printing):
> The Turtles with the best trading records consistently applied the entry rules. The Turtles with the worst records and all those who were dropped from the program failed to enter positions consistently when the rules indicated. [verified]

## ITEM 9 — Faith's anecdotes
**STATUS: (a) and (b) are both VERIFIED. They appear in the 2003 PDF and in the 2007 book's appendix (≈ pp. 273–274).**

### (a) The dismissed first-class Turtle
2007 book wording [verified]:
> One member of the first Turtle class, who was fired from the program before the end of the first year, suspected early on that information had been withheld intentionally from the group and eventually became convinced that there were hidden secrets that Rich would not reveal. That trader could not face up to the simple fact that his poor performance was due to his own doubts and insecurities, which resulted in his inability to follow the rules.

- The 2003 wording is nearly identical ("...intentionally withheld ... hidden secrets which Rich would not reveal. This particular trader could not face up to...").
- **Name:** neither text names him. In the same 2003 PDF, Faith separately attacks an unnamed "Former Turtle" who sold seminars ("he didn't last a full year as a turtle before he was fired"). Forum discussion and Covel-based summaries link that rule-seller to Russell Sands. That link is an inference, not a statement in Faith's text. **Do not use any name.**

### (b) Rule changes meant to reduce risk
2003 PDF (the 2007 appendix matches; key phrases [verified]):
> Another problem is the tendency to want to change the rules. Many of the Turtles, in an effort to reduce the risk of trading the system, changed the rules in subtle ways which sometimes had the opposite of the desired effect. An example: Failing to enter positions as quickly as the rules specify (1 unit every ½ N). While this may seem like a more conservative approach, the reality could be that, for the type of entry system the Turtles used, adding to positions slowly might increase the chance that a retracement would hit the exit stops—resulting in losses—whereas a faster approach might allow the position to weather the retracement without the stops being hit. This subtle change could have a major impact on the profitability of the system during certain market conditions.

Note Faith's hedge: "could be" and "might increase the chance", not "made them more likely".

## ITEM 10 — "...publish my trading rules in the newspaper..."
**STATUS: ORIGINAL SOURCE VERIFIED — Richard Dennis in Jack D. Schwager, *Market Wizards* (1989), in the Dennis interview. The widely circulated wording is NOT the original.**

Original wording, assembled from index snippets of the 1989/1990/1993 editions:
> "I always say that you could publish trading rules in the newspaper and no one would follow them. The key is consistency and discipline. Almost anybody can make up a list of rules that are 80 percent as good as what we taught our people. What they couldn't do is give them the confidence to stick to those rules even when things are going bad."

- Returned verbatim by the index:
  - "...it. I always say that you could publish trading rules in the newspaper and no one would follow them. The..."
  - "...is consistency and discipline. Almost anybody can make up a list of rules that are 80 percent as good as..."
  - "...they couldn't do is give them the confidence to stick to those rules even when things are going bad. How..."
- "what we taught our people": not in the returned snippets. It is supported by the Goodreads *Market Wizards* quotes page and by *Currency Kings*, which quotes "80 percent as good as what we taught our people."
- A search for "publish my trading rules" does NOT match *Market Wizards*.
- The common version ("publish **my** trading rules", "**80%**") is Curtis Faith's rendering in the 2003 PDF ("from Market Wizards, by Jack D. Schwager") and in *Way of the Turtle* (2007). Later books copied it (e.g. *The Ivy Portfolio*). Grant Noble's *The Trader's Edge* prints "publish (my) trading rules", with "my" as a bracketed insertion.
- Page: the Dennis chapter starts at p. 85 in the 1993 HarperBusiness edition (per Wikipedia's citation). The exact page of the quote is not verified.
- Schwager's exact question is not verified. Secondary sources say he asked whether Dennis worried about teaching his methods to others.

## ITEM 11 — Later careers
- **Jerry Parker:** founded Chesapeake Capital Corporation in Richmond, Virginia, in February 1988 and remains Chairman & CEO (chesapeakecapital.com). The firm describes "long-term" systematic trend following and, unusually, applies its trend model to single stocks as well as futures. WSJ 1989: "Chesapeake Capital Corp. is jointly owned by R. Jerry Parker and Russell J. Sands."
- **Liz (Elizabeth) Cheval:** founded EMC Capital Management (Illinois) in 1988 and was its chairman. She died on 9 March 2013 in Beijing after a brain aneurysm, aged 56 (Bloomberg obituary by Laurence Arnold, 18 Mar 2013; Patch; Chicago Tribune death notice). Covel: EMC was run with fellow Turtle Brian Proctor. WSJ 1989 table: 114.1% average annual compound return.
- **Tom Shanks:** traded for Dennis from January 1985 (second class) to May 1988. His firm (QuickSilver Commodities, formed mid-1986) became a registered CTA on 23 Nov 1988 and was renamed Hawksbill Capital Management in Nov 1989 (firm disclosure via IASG/peterknightadvisor). Covel quotes Shanks: "Our trading approach doesn't resemble the systems that we were taught initially except that it thoroughly and consistently embodies the principles of trend following that we were taught." He died in 2026 (Legacy.com obituary published 15 May 2026, "1950 - 2026").
- **Paul Rabar:** founded Rabar Market Research (White Plains, NY). The founding year conflicts: Preqin says 1986; Covel says after the program. The firm's described approach is long-term trend following in which some computer orders were modified by Rabar (Barclay profile). IASG says his systems are partly "trend anticipating" and were "enhanced and modified particularly since the mid-2000's." WSJ 1989: 89.1%.
- **Howard Seidler:** "began trading in January 1984 as one of Richard Dennis' original 'Turtles.' In September 1988 ... founded Saxon [Investment Corporation]". Saxon is "primarily a systematic trend-follower; its trading decisions are generally based on a combination of technical indicators, money management principles and Mr. Seidler's overall experience and judgment" (IASG). WSJ 1989: 64.2%.
- **Similar but not identical to the Turtle rules — VERIFIED as a characterization:**
  - Wikipedia ("Richard Dennis"): these Turtles "began and continued careers as successful commodity trading managers, using techniques similar, but not identical, to the Turtle System."
  - Faith 2003: "Most of the Turtles are now trading even better rules."
  - Eckhardt (Futures 2011): "The successful turtles have branched out widely, but my trading has also changed."
